[9] <Normal> Abstract
[10] <Normal> Background. Disproportionality statistics from spontaneous-reporting databases are distorted by indication, notoriety, and differential reporting. Recent NSCLC studies report large RORs for osimertinib–ILD, osimertinib–QT, and tepotinib–AKI, typically pairing four algorithms with network toxicology but not correcting for the reporting process itself.
[11] <Normal> Methods. Using openFDA (FAERS; 21,054,159 reports, 2026-11-21), we examined six prespecified pairs across four NSCLC agents. We computed ROR, PRR, IC and EBGM, fitted drug-specific empirical nulls from adjudicated negative controls by maximum likelihood (with sample-SD sensitivity), recalibrated and BH-FDR-corrected the estimates, and re-estimated them against pooled active comparators. Monte Carlo simulations characterized type-I error, power and model misspecification. Network proximity (ChEMBL, Open Targets, STRING) served as a prespecified discriminating test against 10,000 degree-matched permutations. Reporting followed READUS-PV.
[12] <Normal> Results. Naive concordance flagged 6/6 pairs but also 37/140 negative controls (26%; median ROR 0.86), invalidating the theoretical null. Under a true null, the naive false-positive rate was 92–99% versus approximately 5% after calibration (84% power at ROR 2). Calibration stayed near-nominal under heavy-tailed bias but degraded when the control panel's dispersion was underestimated (FPR 17–30%), an explicit applicability boundary. Active comparators reduced three ILD signals to class effects: osimertinib–ILD 7.17→0.86, lazertinib–ILD 12.80→1.27, amivantamab–ILD 7.83→1.36. Osimertinib–QT was retained (3.80→3.51); tepotinib–AKI was uncalibratable (4/20 controls) and amivantamab–IRR unstable. Neither external analysis discriminated: no notoriety step aligned with regulatory anchors, network proximity did not differ between retained and attenuated pairs (p = 0.40), and the sole nominal hit vanished after removing borrowed hub genes. In a decision model, calibration-aware triage cut agent-specific action triggers by 83% (6→1) and expected false actions ~19-fold.
[13] <Normal> Conclusions. The six signals resolve into three tiers: one robust agent-specific signal (osimertinib–QT), three ILD class effects, and two unresolved signals (tepotinib–AKI, amivantamab–IRR). Unresolved is not evidence of safety. These are method-layer findings about reading FAERS: the class-effect and QT conclusions match existing labels, and calibration's validity depends on negative-control quality. Comparator-anchored designs with stated limits should precede claims that a FAERS signal differentiates agents within a class.
[14] <Normal> Keywords: pharmacovigilance; empirical calibration; negative controls; disproportionality; active comparator; non-small cell lung cancer; network medicine; osimertinib; tepotinib; READUS-PV
[16] <Normal> 1. Introduction
[17] <Normal> Targeted therapies have reshaped non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treatment, but toxicity remains a decisive constraint on treatment selection. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) and QT prolongation during EGFR inhibition, renal injury during MET inhibition, and infusion-related toxicity during EGFR–MET bispecific antibody treatment are clinically consequential and, for several agents, incompletely characterized at approval. Post-marketing surveillance therefore carries substantial weight in how these agents are monitored and, increasingly, in how they are chosen for individual patients.
[18] <Normal> The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) is the largest publicly accessible source of such evidence, and disproportionality analysis — comparing an observed drug–event count with that expected under independence — is its standard analytic engine. A typical study computes several measures at once: the reporting odds ratio (ROR) [1], proportional reporting ratio (PRR) [2], Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN, information component IC) [3], and empirical Bayes geometric mean (EBGM) [4], treating agreement among them as evidence of robustness. In NSCLC, recent analyses have profiled fourteen agents across five drug classes with this four-algorithm strategy [5], described osimertinib-associated cardiotoxicity with an ROR of 11.2 for QT prolongation (6.6 for other EGFR-TKIs) [6], linked osimertinib to ILD through combined signal detection and network toxicology [7], and proposed a tri-scale framework in which tepotinib-associated AKI yielded an ROR of 20.60 with network and docking support [8].
[19] <Normal> These analyses are valuable hypothesis generators, but they share a structural weakness: disproportionality estimates from spontaneous reports are not unbiased estimates of relative risk, because the reporting process shapes them. Co-indicated agents inherit the same clinical context; newer agents accumulate reports through heightened surveillance and the Weber effect [9]; and publicity after a regulatory communication can inflate a specific drug–event pair without any change in true incidence — the notoriety bias [10]. Under these conditions the theoretical null of ROR = 1 is the wrong reference distribution. In an independent evaluation, 78% of 146 negative-control pairs in FAERS were nominally significant against that null, falling to approximately 4% after empirical calibration [11,12]. Our own panel reproduced the problem: 58.9% of adjudicated inert-control RORs lay below 1 and 26% were nominally flagged, a distribution incompatible with the theoretical null.
[20] <Normal> Empirical calibration addresses this directly. Outcomes with no plausible causal association with the drug measure the bias and residual dispersion of the reporting stream itself; each observed estimate is then recalibrated against that drug-specific empirical null rather than against ROR = 1 [11]. Established in longitudinal observational research, the approach has been transferred to FAERS, where it changed the interpretation of comparative safety signals within a drug class [13]. That application exposed a design sensitivity we treat as a prespecified requirement rather than an afterthought: the conclusion depended on whether the null's dispersion was taken as the sample standard deviation of the control estimates or fitted by maximum likelihood. Without prespecification, calibration can appear to confirm whichever conclusion its implementation happens to produce.
[21] <Normal> A second gap concerns the comparator. Most NSCLC disproportionality studies contrast each drug with the entire database, mixing pharmacology with the reporting behavior of unrelated indications. Active comparators — agents used for the same indication but with a different molecular target — hold clinical context approximately constant, so a signal surviving the contrast is less likely to be a class-wide reporting artifact. Such contrasts retain their own biases, including differences in time on market and reporting intensity, and require cautious interpretation.
[22] <Normal> A third gap is the role of network evidence. Network medicine — the proposition that disease and toxicity phenotypes can be understood through interactome topology — has become a mature framework [14], and network-toxicology analyses are routinely appended to FAERS studies by collecting putative drug targets and toxicity-associated genes and reporting their overlap or hub genes. Rarely is the network result used as a discriminating test: if calibration and comparator adjustment remove a signal as a reporting artifact, mechanistic proximity should be weaker for that signal than for those that survive. Without such a contrast, network evidence is decorative rather than evidentiary, and can give false reassurance when gene sets are not independent of the literature that generated the signal — a failure mode we demonstrate below.
[23] <Normal> We therefore designed a calibration-aware, multi-source pharmacovigilance study of NSCLC targeted therapies with four distinguishing features: (i) drug-specific empirical nulls fitted from adjudicated negative-control outcomes, with estimator and decision rule fixed before outcome analysis; (ii) formal re-estimation against active comparators; (iii) a temporal analysis with negative-control outcomes as a concurrent reference; and (iv) a network-proximity analysis framed as a discriminating test between retained and attenuated signals. We report according to READUS-PV [15,16]. As index pairs we chose signals already published with large uncalibrated RORs, so that the analysis tests the reporting process rather than convenient outcomes.
[25] <Normal> 2. Methods
[26] <Normal> 2.1 Study design and reporting
[27] <Normal> This is a prespecified, methodology-focused observational study of spontaneous reporting data. The analysis plan, drug and event definitions, negative-control adjudication rules, and estimator choices were frozen before any outcome data were accessed (timestamped local deposit with SHA-256 hash chain; Supplementary S1); the study flow is shown in Fig. 1. We make no causal claim. Reporting follows the READUS-PV statement and its explanation and elaboration [15,16]; the completed checklist appears as Supplementary Fig. S1 / Supplementary Table S10.
[28] <Normal> 2.2 Data source and preprocessing
[29] <Normal> We queried the openFDA drug-event application programming interface (api.fda.gov/drug/event.json), the official public interface to FAERS; such interfaces and their research derivatives have become a standard route to spontaneous-report data [17]. The snapshot contained 21,054,159 reports (query date 2026-11-21; the API exposes no fixed closing quarter, so the reporting window is the cumulative archive to that date). Drug exposure was defined by the normalized generic-name field patient.drug.openfda.generic_name, locked before analysis: this field captures both generic and brand-coded reports (osimertinib: 33,148 reports by generic name versus 9,862 by medicinalproduct and 23,974 by brand name), and the choice was fixed to avoid differential misclassification. Aggregate count queries returned the number of reports satisfying a search expression, preserving the 2×2 table counts required for disproportionality without exporting individual records. Because the aggregate interface cannot distinguish suspect from concomitant drugs within a report or perform case-level deduplication, we addressed exposure misclassification in two ways. First, in a prespecified single-variable sensitivity analysis we repeated every query with the reporter's suspect-role flag imposed (patient.drug.drugcharacterization:"1"), restricting exposure to drugs marked suspect and holding all other definitions constant (Supplementary S2). Second, we state deduplication as an explicit limitation: openFDA identifies each submitted report uniquely, and FAERS records subsequent updates as separate reports, so repeated case versions cannot be collapsed in aggregate counts. Adverse events were coded to MedDRA preferred terms (PTs); clinically coherent PTs were grouped into standardized outcome definitions using prespecified lists (Supplementary S2): ILD = interstitial lung disease, pneumonitis, interstitial pneumonitis, pulmonary fibrosis, acute interstitial pneumonia; QT = electrocardiogram QT prolonged, long QT syndrome; AKI = acute kidney injury, renal impairment, blood creatinine increased, renal failure acute; infusion-related reaction = infusion related reaction(s). All queries and derived counts are reproducible from the scripts in the deposited repository. Because only aggregate, de-identified counts from a public interface were used, no ethics approval or informed consent was required (see Declarations).
[30] <Normal> 2.3 Index drugs, outcomes, and active comparators
[31] <Normal> Table 1 lists the index agents and their six prespecified index pairs. Active comparators were agents approved for the same NSCLC indication with a distinct or shared molecular target, chosen before outcome analysis: osimertinib → gefitinib, erlotinib, afatinib, dacomitinib, lazertinib (EGFR-TKI class); tepotinib → capmatinib (savolitinib was removed because it had zero reports under the locked generic-name field); lazertinib → osimertinib, gefitinib, afatinib; amivantamab → capmatinib, tepotinib (MET-directed). We analyzed each index pair both against the full database (naive) and against its pooled active-comparator set.
[32] <Normal> 2.4 Four disproportionality algorithms
[33] <Normal> For each drug–event pair we computed the ROR with 95% CI [1], PRR with χ² [2], BCPNN information component (IC with IC₀₂₅) [3], and EBGM (with EBGM₀₅) [4]. Prespecified signal thresholds were ROR lower 95% bound > 1 with a ≥ 3; PRR ≥ 2, χ² ≥ 4, a ≥ 3; IC₀₂₅ > 0; and EBGM₀₅ > 2. Naive concordance meant meeting all four thresholds. These four statistics are correlated functions of the same 2×2 table — as emphasized in comparisons of disproportionality measures [18] and in the original data-mining literature [19] — so their concordance is not an independent replication of a signal; we report it only for comparability with prior NSCLC literature.
[34] <Normal> 2.5 Negative-control outcomes and empirical calibration
[35] <Normal> We selected negative controls per drug in three prespecified classes: (N1) pharmacologically inert PTs with no label or published association with the drug or its class (e.g., cataract, osteoarthritis, carpal tunnel syndrome, inguinal hernia, diverticulitis, cholelithiasis); (N2) events common in the NSCLC population but lacking a mechanistic link to the drug's target (contextual sentinels); and (N3) temporal controls. Negative-control outcomes are well established for detecting unmeasured confounding and other biases in observational research [20,21], with a growing methodological literature in pharmacovigilance [22]. We decontaminated candidate controls against the product label, pivotal randomized trials, and published meta-analyses, removing any candidate with an on-label mention or published positive association before fitting. Control disposition is in Supplementary S2.
[36] <Normal> For each drug we fitted the empirical null as ln(ROR)_i ~ N(μ_d, σ_d² + SE_i²) by maximum likelihood, separating the controls' sampling error from systematic dispersion (primary estimator). Empirical-null calibration of effect estimates and confidence intervals is established in population-level observational studies [23] and has been extended to adaptive, bias-corrected safety surveillance [24]. A sample-standard-deviation fit served as a prespecified conservative sensitivity analysis. We recalibrated each estimate as ln(ROR)_cal = ln(ROR)_obs − μ_d with SE_cal = √(SE² + σ_d²), converted it to a two-sided calibrated p-value, and applied Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across the six index pairs. A pair was classified robust only if (a) its calibrated 95% lower bound exceeded one under both estimators and (b) its BH q < 0.05 under the primary estimator. We required at least ten usable inert controls for a stable null fit. We adopted this threshold after observing boundary solutions (σ = 0) in the small panels; it is therefore a data-informed analysis rule, reported transparently as a deviation in Supplementary S1, not a criterion fixed before outcome access, and we apply it as an explicit quality flag. Only osimertinib (N1 = 17) met the threshold; tepotinib N1 (n = 4) was not estimable, and lazertinib (n = 5, boundary σ = 0) and amivantamab (n = 7) fell below it. We report calibrated estimates for the latter two but flag them low-confidence; their calibrated significance is not used to support the final tier assignment, which rests primarily on active-comparator re-estimation and estimate stability (§3.7). Hence the class-effect conclusion does not depend on the small panels: all three ILD pairs attenuate against active comparators irrespective of their N1 calibration. Fifteen label-established positive controls across drugs confirmed that calibration did not remove genuine effects (14/15 retained). Because such controls have large true effects and are retained almost by construction, we ran a Monte Carlo simulation to characterize type-I error, power, and behavior at weak-to-moderate effect sizes. Using the fitted null parameters and SEs resampled from the six index pairs, we simulated ln(ROR)_obs ~ N(μ + δ, σ² + SE²) over 20,000 replicates (fixed seed 20261121) under (i) δ = 0 to measure type-I error of the naive versus calibrated test, (ii) a grid of true RORs from 1.2 to 6 to trace calibrated power, and (iii) a 15-control panel with deliberately modest true effects (ROR 1.4–2.5) to test directly for over-shrinkage. Two null families spanning the empirical bracket were used: osimertinib N1 (μ = −0.836, σ = 0.214; primary) and N2 (μ = +0.538, σ = 1.435; upper-bound confounded sentinel). This simulation generates data from the same normal null that calibration fits; it is therefore a mechanism demonstration under the model's assumptions, not an empirical validation of those assumptions. To test behavior off-model we added a misspecification stress test in which the true bias distribution was varied away from the fitted normal — inflated dispersion (σ × 1.5, × 2), heavy-tailed t(5) bias, and pairwise null-center drift (τ = 0.15, 0.30) — while calibration still applied the fitted parameters (Supplementary S15, Supplementary Fig. S2). Full specification is in Supplementary S11 and S15.
[37] <Normal> 2.6 Temporal (notoriety) analysis
[38] <Normal> We constructed quarterly reporting-fraction series for each index pair from the earliest available quarter. For each drug and quarter q we computed the index-event reporting fraction a_q / n_q (a = index-event reports, n = all reports for that drug in q), the concurrent negative-control fraction c_q / n_q (c = pooled N1 control reports), and their difference d_q = (a_q − c_q)/n_q. We excluded quarters with fewer than 20 drug reports. Regulatory anchor quarters came from FDA labels and DailyMed archives (osimertinib initial approval 2015Q4 and first-line expansion 2018Q2; tepotinib and amivantamab accelerated approvals 2021Q2; Supplementary S3). Because ILD and QT warnings were present in the initial osimertinib label, no single sentinel communication exists for those pairs; we therefore used a data-driven change-point estimator (the split maximizing |mean(d_after) − mean(d_before)|, requiring ≥4 quarters per side) and assessed its alignment with the anchor quarters rather than imposing a date. We defined a notoriety pattern a priori as a positive step in d_q coincident with a regulatory anchor and absent from the negative-control series.
[39] <Normal> 2.7 Network evidence as a prespecified discriminating test
[40] <Normal> We obtained drug targets from ChEMBL mechanism records [25] (molecular_mechanism = 1): EGFR (CHEMBL203) for osimertinib and lazertinib; MET (CHEMBL3717) for tepotinib; and EGFR + MET for amivantamab. Toxicity-associated gene sets came from Open Targets disease–target associations [26] (ILD MONDO_0015925; prolonged QT HP_0001657; AKI MONDO_0002492; drug hypersensitivity EFO_1002004) combined with a curated, literature-anchored core set; Open Targets association scores were low (≈0.004), so the curated core carried most of the weight — a limitation we return to below. A STRING induced interactome [27] on the union of all genes (combined score ≥ 0.4 primary, ≥ 0.7 sensitivity) provided the background graph. For each pair we computed the network proximity d_c = mean over drug targets s of min over toxicity genes t of shortest_path(s,t) (Cheng et al. closest-proximity measure [28]), with significance against 10,000 degree-matched random gene sets (fixed seed). This measure has a structural limitation here: four of six index drugs have a single primary target (EGFR or MET), for which the "mean over s" reduces to one gene and the measure has low resolution; under the doubly restricted scenario this produced null-variance collapse (uninterpretable z values). We therefore treat the negative network result as suggestive but qualified by the measure's resolution limit, and do not generalize it to all network-medicine approaches (Discussion). The prespecified discriminating test compared proximity between retained and attenuated pairs; because group sizes were 3 versus 3 and a Mann–Whitney test cannot attain two-sided p < 0.05 at that size, we used an exact label-permutation test over all 20 possible group assignments. To probe circularity we repeated the analysis under four scenarios: S1 (all genes), S2 (primary targets only), S3 (organ-specific toxicity core), and S4 (both restrictions). Network analyses were performed after calibration, with group labels fixed by the rule in §2.5.
[41] <Normal> 2.8 Statistical environment and version locking
[42] <Normal> We locked all external data sources and software versions at analysis time (2026-11-21) to make the results exactly reproducible; the full lock record is in Supplementary S12. In brief: FAERS aggregate counts came from the openFDA drug-event API (cumulative archive 2004 to query date; quarterly updates; the API exposes neither a fixed closing quarter nor its MedDRA version); drug targets from the ChEMBL API, release ChEMBL_37 (2026-05-01); toxicity gene sets from the Open Targets Platform GraphQL API, v26.9.0 with data release 2026-09; and the interactome from the STRING REST API, v12.5, queried at combined score ≥ 0.4 (primary) and ≥ 0.7 (sensitivity). Analysis used Python 3.13.14 with pandas 2.3.3, numpy 2.5.4, scipy 1.18.1, networkx 3.7, matplotlib 3.11.2, and requests 2.34.2. All stochastic procedures used fixed seeds: 20261121 for the calibration simulation (§2.5) and 20261121 for degree-matched permutation in the network analysis (§2.7). Two-sided α = 0.05 unless stated. Code, frozen definitions, derived tables, and the version lock file are archived as described in the Availability of data and materials section. Because openFDA does not disclose the MedDRA version in its API response and quarterly snapshots are cumulative, exact re-derivation requires re-querying on a stated date; we therefore report the query date and total report count alongside every count. We record the absence of a fixed closing quarter and of a disclosed MedDRA version as a reproducibility limitation (Discussion, Limitations). Analysis code was developed with generative AI assistance and executed under the author's direction; the deposited scripts reproduce all reported outputs exactly (see Declarations).
[44] <Normal> 3. Results
[45] <Normal> 3.1 Cohort and report characteristics
[46] <Normal> The snapshot comprised 21,054,159 FAERS reports. Report volumes for the index drugs were uneven: osimertinib 33,148, amivantamab 3,858, lazertinib 865, tepotinib 691 — a roughly 48-fold range that proved decisive for calibration feasibility (§3.3; Table 2). Demographics and reporting-intensity details are in Supplementary S4.
[47] <Normal> 3.2 The theoretical null fails for inert outcomes
[48] <Normal> All 6/6 index pairs met the naive four-algorithm concordance criterion (Table 3). The same criterion also flagged 37 of 140 adjudicated negative controls (26%), and 58.9% of control ROR point estimates lay below 1 (median 0.86). Events selected a priori to be inert were systematically displaced from ROR = 1, shifted downward and dispersed beyond what the theoretical null allows (Fig. 2A). This validates the premise of empirical calibration and shows why naive concordance is not evidence of a true signal.
[49] <Normal> Suspect-role sensitivity. Restricting exposure to reports in which the drug was marked suspect changed exposure counts by at most 0.23% (osimertinib 33,148 → 33,111; tepotinib 691 → 691; lazertinib 865 → 863; amivantamab 3,858 → 3,857) and index-pair RORs by at most 2.38% (largest, lazertinib–ILD 12.80 → 12.49). The suspect-only analysis changed no robustness call, null parameter, active-comparator attenuation call, or positive-control retention (Supplementary Table S2b). The near-equivalence arises because openFDA's normalized generic_name field is populated predominantly for the report's suspect drug; in this API the two exposure definitions are almost collinear. Concomitant-drug contamination is therefore unlikely to drive our estimates, though case-level deduplication remains unaddressed (§4, Limitations).
[50] <Normal> 3.3 Calibration and estimator sensitivity
[51] <Normal> Drug-specific empirical nulls varied substantially: μ ranged from −0.835 (osimertinib N1) to +0.545 (tepotinib N2), and σ from a boundary 0 (lazertinib N1, unreliable) to 1.435 (osimertinib N2) (Table 4; Fig. 2B). After calibration, 5/6 pairs retained a 95% lower bound above one under both the ML and sample-SD fits and were FDR-significant; 3/6 were bracket-stable under both the N1 and N2 null families. These counts must be read with the panel quality flag of §2.5: only the two osimertinib pairs were calibrated on a panel meeting the ≥10-control threshold (N1 = 17), so their calibrated significance is the robust subset; the three pairs on small panels (lazertinib n = 5, amivantamab n = 7) are low-confidence on calibration and are not used for tiering. Tepotinib–AKI was the exception: only 4 of 20 inert controls had non-zero counts at tepotinib's report volume, the N1 null could not be fitted, and its calibrated value was not estimable. Positive controls were preserved (14/15), but these are large-effect, label-established associations, so this alone is weak evidence against over-shrinkage. The simulation (Fig. 6, Supplementary S11) characterizes the procedure's behavior under its own assumptions. Under a true null, the naive test produced a false-positive rate of 99.4% (N1) and 91.6% (N2) at α = 0.05 — the reporting process alone makes almost any pair "significant" — whereas calibration returned the rate to 5.1% and 5.0%, that is, nominal (BH-FDR: naive 99.4%/91.5%, calibrated 0.0%/0.0%). Against the primary N1 null, calibrated power was 84% at a true ROR of 2, 99% at ROR 3, and ≥99% at ROR ≥ 4, versus 42% at ROR 1.5 and 12% at ROR 1.2 — calibration removes bias-driven false positives without materially sacrificing power for genuine moderate-to-strong effects. The borderline 15-control panel (true ROR 1.4–2.5) retained 9/15 under the primary null, close to the naive 8/15, directly refuting systematic over-shrinkage. The high-dispersion N2 null behaved differently: power was only 24% even at ROR 6 and 0/15 borderline controls survived, while the naive test retained 14/15 — 14 false positives. This is an applicability boundary, not a failure: a sentinel panel with σ = 1.435 is too dispersed to support a calibrated positive claim, and it correctly withholds significance. Together, these results show that the procedure is selective (suppressing spurious signals) yet non-destructive (preserving real moderate effects) when the control panel is well populated, and self-limiting when it is not. Because inert controls were under-reported (μ < 0 for the N1 panels), calibration raised ROR point estimates (e.g., osimertinib–ILD 7.17 → 16.53); the loss of significance arose from variance inflation and from the comparator step, not from a fall in the point estimate.
[52] <Normal> Because the simulation above generates data from the fitted null, we additionally stress-tested calibration off-model (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary S15). Calibrated FPR stayed near nominal when the true bias was heavy-tailed (t(5): 5.1%), confirming robustness to outlier-driven reporting. It degraded, however, when the fitted null understated dispersion (true σ × 1.5 → FPR 17%; × 2 → 30%) or when the null center drifted across pairs (τ = 0.15 → 10%; τ = 0.30 → 23%); an oracle using the true parameters held at approximately 5% throughout. This quantifies our applicability boundary: calibration is reliable when the negative-control panel is both well populated (§2.5 threshold) and a faithful measure of the reporting stream. When the panel mis-estimates σ or μ, calibration inherits that error and can become anti-conservative. This is a boundary, not a refutation, and it is why we report μ, σ, and usable-control counts alongside every calibrated estimate.
[53] <Normal> 3.4 Active-comparator re-estimation separates class effects from drug-specific signals
[54] <Normal> Re-estimation against pooled active comparators attenuated 3/6 pairs to non-significance (95% lower bound ≤ 1; Fig. 3, Table 5). All three were ILD: osimertinib–ILD ROR 7.17 → 0.86 (0.79–0.93), lazertinib–ILD 12.80 → 1.27 (0.92–1.75), and amivantamab–ILD 7.83 → 1.36 (1.00–1.86). Their apparently large database-wide RORs therefore reflect an EGFR/EGFR–MET class effect rather than agent-specific toxicity. Drug-specific signals were retained by contrast: osimertinib–QT 3.80 → 3.51 (2.62–4.69) and tepotinib–AKI 8.68 → 2.92 (2.17–3.94). Amivantamab–infusion-related reaction increased against MET comparators (80.8 → 1542, 96–24,683) because the comparators had zero reports for that event; we flag this estimate as unstable and interpret it qualitatively (antibody-specific toxicity) rather than numerically.
[55] <Normal> 3.5 Temporal analysis shows no notoriety pattern
[56] <Normal> Across 100 drug-quarter observations, no index pair exhibited the prespecified notoriety pattern (Fig. 4, Supplementary S6). Change points in the index-minus-control reporting fraction were non-positive or negligible for all pairs (largest positive increment +0.008 for lazertinib–ILD, at 2025Q3 with no regulatory anchor; principal changes were negative: osimertinib–ILD −0.053 at 2019Q4, tepotinib–AKI −0.180 at 2023Q1, amivantamab–IRR −0.173 at 2022Q3). ILD and QT warnings were present from initial approval, and the detected change points post-dated the anchor quarters; the temporal evidence therefore does not support notoriety-driven inflation of these signals. The analysis was uninformative for tepotinib (its N1 control series was entirely zero) and imprecise for amivantamab (11 control events) — limitations we report rather than gloss over.
[57] <Normal> 3.6 Network evidence fails the prespecified discriminating test
[58] <Normal> Contrary to expectation, network proximity did not separate retained from attenuated pairs: median z = −0.71 (retained) versus −0.92 (attenuated), mean z difference −0.26, exact label-permutation p = 0.40, consistent at STRING score thresholds 0.4 and 0.7 (Fig. 5). The only nominally significant pair, tepotinib–AKI (z = −2.54, p = 0.021), lost significance when the six hub genes imported from the prior tepotinib network-toxicology report [8] were removed from the target set (z = +0.67, p = 1.00, scenario S2) and when an organ-specific toxicity core was used (z = +0.62, p = 0.89, S3). Under the doubly restricted scenario S4 the null variance collapsed for single-gene target sets, producing uninterpretable z values (Fig. 5 inset). Amivantamab–IRR showed positive z (+0.80), consistent with the inapplicability of topological proximity to immune-mediated infusion reactions. We therefore claim no network-level mechanistic support for any surviving signal. The negative result instead demonstrates the circularity risk when "mechanistic" gene sets are derived from the same literature that produced the pharmacovigilance signal. Four of six drugs have a single target (reducing the proximity measure's resolution), and the toxicity sets were largely literature-derived (§2.7); we therefore interpret this as a failure of this specific metric-and-annotation combination in pharmacovigilance add-ons, not as evidence that network proximity is uninformative in general.
[59] <Normal> 3.7 Final cross-source tiering
[60] <Normal> Integrating calibration robustness, comparator direction, and estimate stability (network evidence excluded after §3.6), one pair was Tier A (FDR-significant, robust under both estimators, retained against active comparators, and a stable estimate): osimertinib–QT; three were Tier B (robust but attenuated to class effects): osimertinib–ILD, lazertinib–ILD, amivantamab–ILD; and two were Tier C: tepotinib–AKI (calibration not estimable at current report volume) and amivantamab–IRR (comparator estimate unstable because the MET comparator pool had zero IRR reports, yielding an upper confidence bound of 24,683). Requiring estimate stability for Tier A prevents a zero-denominator artifact from being promoted to a top-tier signal (Table 6). Tier assignment describes the evidential weight of the reporting statistics, not clinical absence of risk: Tier B signals remain real toxicities warranting class-level monitoring, and Tier C signals are unresolved because the reporting data cannot calibrate them. Insufficient evidence is not evidence of safety; on the basis of this analysis neither tepotinib–AKI nor amivantamab–IRR should prompt relaxed surveillance.
[61] <Normal> 3.8 Decision consequences of naive versus calibration-aware triage
[62] <Normal> To translate the statistical results into regulatory consequences, we modeled the number of pairs that would remain eligible for an agent-specific safety action (a label-change discussion or product-specific monitoring decision) at each analysis stage (Fig. 7, Supplementary S14). A naive four-algorithm screen would advance all six pairs; requiring an estimable empirical calibration removed tepotinib–AKI (five remaining); active-comparator re-estimation reclassified the three ILD pairs as class effects (two remaining, managed by class-level rather than agent-specific monitoring); and requiring estimate stability removed amivantamab–IRR, leaving one pair (osimertinib–QT). The calibration-aware pathway therefore reduced agent-specific decision triggers by 83% (6 → 1) without discarding the class-level monitoring message. The simulation quantifies the corresponding false-action burden: under the fitted reporting-process null, a naive screen of six pairs would be expected to generate 5.7 false agent-specific actions (mean false-positive rate 95.5%), versus 0.30 after calibration (5.1%) — approximately a 19-fold reduction in expected spurious decisions. This is a decision-model calculation, not an observed count of regulatory events, and class-effect reclassification retains a real monitoring recommendation; its purpose is to show that the choice of statistical rule has a large, quantifiable downstream cost.
[64] <Normal> 4. Discussion
[65] <Normal> Principal finding. Naive four-algorithm concordance flagged all six published, high-profile NSCLC targeted-therapy safety signals. Calibration plus active-comparator re-estimation resolved them into three evidential tiers: one robust, stable agent-specific signal (osimertinib–QT, Tier A); three ILD signals (Tier B) that are genuine but reflect an EGFR/EGFR–MET class effect rather than agent-specific toxicity; and two unresolved signals (Tier C) — tepotinib–AKI because calibration was not estimable at current report volume, amivantamab–IRR because the comparator estimate was unstable. Tier C denotes insufficient evidence, not absence of risk; neither unresolved signal should be read as reassuring. Uncalibrated concordance overstates agent-specific differentiation in this therapeutic area: the statistical properties of the reporting stream, not pharmacology alone, determine which signals appear. This is a method-layer finding. The class-effect interpretation of ILD and the agent-specific QT liability of osimertinib are already reflected in current labels; our contribution is to show what evidential weight the reporting statistics can and cannot bear.
[66] <Normal> Relation to prior NSCLC FAERS studies. The studies we tested [5–8] are valid hypothesis generators. Their large RORs, however, were measured against a null that our negative controls show to be wrong; their four-algorithm agreement is mathematically correlated, not independent; and their network mapping was not used as a discriminating test. We do not dispute the clinical plausibility of ILD, QT prolongation, or AKI for these agents — ILD is a recognized class effect of EGFR inhibition and QT a labeled risk of osimertinib. We dispute the evidential weight assigned to the uncalibrated statistics as evidence of agent-specific differentiation.
[67] <Normal> Clinical anchoring — what these RORs are and are not. A reporting odds ratio measures the relative disproportionate reporting of an event for a drug within a reporting database; it is not an incidence rate, a risk ratio, or a hazard ratio, and it cannot be read as the probability that a treated patient will develop the toxicity. None of the RORs here — including the retained osimertinib–QT estimate of 3.51 — quantifies clinical risk magnitude. We therefore anchor the clinically meaningful statements in this paper to evidence that already exists outside FAERS: ILD is an established class effect of EGFR-directed therapy, reflected in class labeling and clinical guidelines, and QT prolongation is a labeled, monitorable liability of osimertinib. Our calibration and comparator results are consistent with those anchors rather than replacing them; they show that uncalibrated FAERS statistics cannot reliably rank agents within a class on these toxicities. Absolute risks and monitoring thresholds should come from clinical-trial and label data, not from the reporting statistics reported here. We make this explicit so that the "one agent-specific signal" result is not mistaken for a claim that the other five toxicities are clinically unimportant.
[68] <Normal> Estimator sensitivity as a result. Under both the conservative sample-SD fit and the ML fit, 5/6 pairs survived; our robustness rule requires both. The panel quality flag (§2.5) tempers this apparent stability: the two estimators agree mainly where the panel is well populated (osimertinib N1, n = 17), whereas the low-confidence panels (lazertinib n = 5 with boundary σ = 0; amivantamab n = 7; tepotinib N1 not estimable) contribute calibrated values we do not use for tiering. The CNS re-analysis [13] is informative in this respect: estimator ambiguity there reversed the conclusion from 0/32 to 6/32 surviving signals. Estimator agreement is a property of panel quality, not a guarantee of calibration validity. We argue for prespecifying the estimator and reporting the minimum usable control count as a reporting standard, and for treating calibration significance from small panels as exploratory.
[69] <Normal> What the simulation adds to the positive-control argument. Retaining 14/15 label-established controls is reassuring but nearly guaranteed: those effects lie far above any decision threshold. The Monte Carlo study (§3.3, Fig. 6) makes the underlying mechanism explicit. Under the fitted reporting process the naive disproportionality test is structurally invalid — a 92–99% false-positive rate under a true null — which is the statistical mechanism behind the "FAERS inflation" critique; calibration restores nominal type-I error and retains approximately 84% power at ROR 2. We are careful about what this establishes. Because the simulation generates data from the normal null that calibration fits, it demonstrates correct behavior conditional on the model, not empirical validation of the model; it upgrades the positive-control claim from an assertion to a quantified property under stated assumptions, and no further. The N2 upper-bound scenario (24% power at ROR 6; no borderline control survives) and the off-model misspecification test (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary S15) delineate the price: with a dispersed or mis-specified panel, calibration becomes conservative (N2) or can lose type-I-error control when σ is understated or μ drifts (FPR up to 30%). The practical implication is unchanged but better founded: the credibility of a calibrated signal depends on negative-control panel quality. Reporting μ, σ, and the usable-control count (Table 4) is therefore not optional detail but the information a reader needs to judge how much weight the calibration can bear.
[70] <Normal> Active comparators and residual confounding. Active comparators remove the shared indication but not differential reporting intensity, time on market, or channel bias; the underlying concern is selection bias in the comparator contrast [29]. This matters here because the comparison is not balanced on the very factors that drive reporting: osimertinib (first-line, 2015 approval, 33,148 reports) is contrasted with earlier-generation EGFR-TKIs used largely in later lines and on different market trajectories, and FAERS carries no line-of-therapy field with which to match them. We cannot exclude, therefore, that part of the ILD attenuation reflects differential reporting opportunity rather than a pure class effect. Two observations mitigate but do not eliminate this concern: the suspect-role sensitivity analysis changed no attenuation call (§3.2), and attenuation is directionally consistent across three independent index pairs (osimertinib, lazertinib, amivantamab ILD) against different comparator pools, which would be unlikely if a single agent's reporting trajectory drove it. A definitive separation would require case-level, line-of-therapy or exposure-duration data unavailable in FAERS; a time-on-market-matched sensitivity analysis is a concrete next step. The three ILD pairs are best read as class effects: clinically meaningful (supporting class-level pulmonary monitoring) but not absence of risk. Conversely, retained signals such as osimertinib–QT are consistent with the labeled, agent-specific QT liability and with the comparative ROR of 6.6 versus other EGFR-TKIs reported previously [6].
[71] <Normal> Network and temporal evidence as negative controls. Both prespecified external-validity analyses failed to discriminate. The temporal analysis found no notoriety steps aligned with regulatory anchors; the network analysis not only failed to discriminate but proved circular at the one nominally significant pair. This is a concrete demonstration of a known failure mode in multi-source network pharmacology: when the mechanistic gene set is derived from the same literature that generated the pharmacovigilance signal, apparent validation is guaranteed. Degree matching mitigates hub-degree bias but cannot correct annotation or circularity bias. We therefore assign inferential weight to statistical de-biasing (calibration, active comparators) and treat post hoc network layering as hypothesis-generating at best — a position directly responsive to critiques of transparency and "spin" in disproportionality research [30,31]. We limit the scope of this conclusion deliberately. The network layer that failed here used a single closest-path proximity measure with poor resolution for the single-target drugs that dominate this set, and toxicity gene sets that were largely literature-derived (Open Targets scores ≈ 0.004). Our result speaks against this combination of metric and annotation practice in pharmacovigilance add-ons, not against network medicine as a discipline, which has produced validated successes elsewhere [14]. Recast positively for a network-medicine audience, the contribution is a discriminating-validation protocol — prespecify the network result as a test, then remove literature-borrowed hubs and check that the signal survives — and a demonstration of what happens without it. Degree-matched nulls and independent gene-set sources are the natural remedies; we set them out as requirements rather than claiming the failure is universal.
[72] <Normal> Limitations. Spontaneous reports cannot establish incidence or causality; PT-based outcome definitions lack imaging or laboratory adjudication; negative controls are a mechanistic judgment, not an established fact. Raw spontaneous-report counts are not directly interpretable as frequency or risk [32]. A specific data-integrity limitation is that openFDA aggregate counts cannot distinguish suspect from concomitant drugs at the report level or collapse repeated versions of the same case: FAERS records subsequent updates as separate reports with unique identifiers, so case-level deduplication as recommended by READUS-PV could not be implemented. Our suspect-role sensitivity analysis (§3.2) shows that the role distinction changes estimates by <2.4% here, but residual duplication could still inflate counts for frequently updated reports and cannot be quantified from aggregate queries; a case-level re-analysis on the quarterly ASCII files is the appropriate remedy. The API snapshot also lacks a fixed closing quarter; calibration was infeasible for low-volume agents and we report that boundary rather than impute; the temporal analysis was uninformative where control series were empty; network target sets are incomplete and biased toward well-studied proteins; and this is a single-database analysis without EudraVigilance or VigiBase replication. We did not register the study prospectively: the analysis plan was frozen locally before outcome access with a timestamped hash chain, and we use the phrase "prespecified plan frozen before outcome access" rather than "pre-registered."
[73] <Normal> Replication route in other spontaneous-reporting systems. This is a single-database analysis; triangulation across independent reporting systems is the natural next test of the calibration framework. We set out a concrete route (details in Supplementary S13). EudraVigilance exposes aggregated individual-case reports through the EMA's adrreports.eu portal, queryable by active substance or product with tabulations by reaction term, age group, sex, seriousness, and geographic origin and updated monthly. Because the portal returns aggregated counts by substance and MedDRA term, it can reconstruct the four 2×2 cells for our index pairs and, critically, for the same negative-control PTs, allowing the empirical null to be refitted from an independent reporting population. The WHO system offers two tiers: VigiAccess provides public aggregate counts by drug and reaction (without age or country detail), while case-level extracts require an application to the Uppsala Monitoring Centre (access level 2A limited dataset or 2B full dataset, no free text) subject to the UMC Caveat agreement and, for non-members, a fee; VigiBase currently holds over 40 million case reports from more than 160 countries [33]. Either tier permits the same disproportionality-and-calibration pipeline; case-level access additionally enables the case-version deduplication that the openFDA aggregate interface precludes here, directly addressing our principal data-integrity limitation. A third, immediately executable replication uses the FAERS quarterly ASCII files themselves: rebuilding the same counts from the DEMO/DRUG/REAC tables with the FDA's recommended case + FDA_DT deduplication would quantify how much residual duplication affects our estimates. The prespecified decision rule and negative-control lists are source-agnostic and deposited with the analysis plan, so these replications require no redesign. We expect effect attenuation under calibration to reproduce, but not the absolute RORs: reporting populations, MedDRA release, and background reporting intensity differ — which is precisely why the comparison tests the method rather than the specific numbers.
[74] <Normal> Translation. For clinicians, ILD monitoring should be treated as a class-wide EGFR/EGFR–MET phenomenon, whereas osimertinib–QT is the signal our analysis supports as robustly agent-specific (Tier A). The three ILD signals are real class effects; the two Tier C signals are unresolved. Operationally, this supports embedding ILD surveillance in a unified monitoring pathway for the EGFR/EGFR–MET class rather than risk-stratifying individual agents by their FAERS signal ranking — the class-level monitoring message is preserved even though the apparent between-agent differences are not. Tepotinib–AKI remains biologically plausible but uncalibrated at current report volume, and amivantamab–IRR is a recognized antibody-specific reaction whose disproportionality estimate is too unstable to rank. Neither should drive agent differentiation on present evidence, and their Tier C status must not be read as reassurance: these signals warrant continued surveillance, and insufficient evidence is not evidence of safety. For regulators and methodologists, calibration and active comparators — with the applicability boundary stated — should precede any claim that a FAERS signal differentiates agents within a class. In our decision model the calibration-aware pathway cut agent-specific decision triggers by 83% (6 → 1) and the expected false-action burden roughly 19-fold relative to a naive screen (§3.8, Fig. 7), while preserving the class-level monitoring message.
